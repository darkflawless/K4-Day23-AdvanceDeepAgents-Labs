# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Algorithms, Rewards, Emergent Behaviour, and Evaluation

## TL;DR

- The field has moved from actor–critic RLHF/PPO [1] to *critic-free group-relative* policy optimisation: GRPO removes the value network and estimates advantages from the mean reward of a sampled group [2][3], and downstream variants (Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO, VAPO, REINFORCE++/RLOO) refine advantage normalisation, clipping granularity, and KL placement rather than the basic RL recipe [4][5][6][7][8][9][10].
- Verifiable, rule-based rewards (RLVR) are now the default for math, code, and formal domains because deterministic checkers resist reward hacking better than learned reward models [11][12][13]; the dominant open problems are reward hacking/over-optimisation [14][15][16], the cost of process supervision [17][18], and verifier noise [19].
- Outcome-only RL elicits long chain-of-thought, self-verification, reflection, and the celebrated "aha moment" [11], but the claim that it *expands* capability beyond the base model is sharply contested: pass@k evidence suggests reweighting rather than expansion [20], while prolonged RL [21], chain-validating metrics [22] and saturation-aware analyses [23] support genuine-but-masked boundary expansion.
- A recurring engineering finding across all sub-literatures is **entropy/diversity collapse**: RLVR reliably improves pass@1 while eroding high-k coverage, driving a whole family of entropy-control, exploration, and gradient-stabilisation remedies [24][25][26][23].
- Evaluation is now the weakest link: headline RLVR gains are confounded by budget mismatch, calibration drift, and benchmark contamination (6%–40% inflation estimated across 55 studies) [27][28][29][30], and the pass@k "crossover" inference rests on at least five unaudited premises [31][32].

## Background

**What the topic is.** Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning is the practice of post-training a language model with a reward signal in order to improve multi-step problem solving — mathematics, code, formal proof, logic, and increasingly agentic tasks. Unlike classical deep RL, the "environment" is largely the model's own token stream: the policy emits a chain-of-thought (CoT) plus a final answer, receives a scalar (or step-level) reward, and is updated with a policy-gradient objective, usually with a KL penalty to a reference policy to prevent drift.

**The canonical pipeline.** The template was set by InstructGPT, which established supervised fine-tuning (SFT) → reward model trained on human rankings → PPO against that reward model with a per-token KL penalty, finding that a 1.3B aligned model was preferred over a 175B unaligned one [1]. Direct Preference Optimization later showed the RLHF objective can be reparameterised so the policy is optimised directly on preference pairs, bypassing both reward modelling and online sampling [33] — a lineage that remains relevant as the "RL-free" baseline.

**Why it matters now.** The inflection point was DeepSeek-R1-Zero: pure RL with *rule-based, outcome-only* rewards and no SFT produced spontaneous long CoT, self-verification, reflection, and a marked increase in the token "wait" during reflection, lifting AIME 2024 pass@1 from 15.6% to 71.0% [11]. This demonstrated that a cheap, deterministic reward could substitute for expensive human preference data on reasoning tasks, and triggered an unusually fast-moving open research literature — including an ecosystem of open RL systems, surveys [34][35], and reproducibility critiques [36][27]. The topic now sits at the intersection of three questions: which algorithm, which reward, and how to know whether the gain is real.

## Algorithmic design space: from PPO to group-relative and sequence-level objectives

**The actor–critic foundation and its costs.** PPO with a learned value function was the default [1], but it is expensive: the value network is typically policy-sized, adding memory and compute, and value estimation is noisy on long CoT where sequences run to tens of thousands of tokens [3]. This cost is the direct motivation for the critic-free turn.

**GRPO: group-relative, critic-free.** DeepSeekMath's Group Relative Policy Optimization samples a group of G outputs per question, uses the group's mean reward as the baseline (standardised by group standard deviation), and adds an unbiased KL estimator directly to the loss rather than into the reward [2][3]. It reduced model copies and reported GSM8K 82.9%→88.2%, MATH 46.8%→51.7% [2]. Crucially, GRPO's advantage is computed *within* each group, aligning the objective with the comparative nature of reward signals [3].

**Debiasing GRPO.** "Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training" identified two biases baked into the GRPO objective: a response-level *length* bias (dividing by |o_i| inflates the gradient contribution of incorrect long responses, which partly explains the observed "long-CoT emergence") and a question-level *difficulty* bias from standard-deviation normalisation, which over-weights low-variance prompts [4]. Removing both recovers an unbiased PPO-style objective; the minimalist recipe reached 43.3% on AIME 2024 for a 7B model in 27 hours on 8×A100 [4]. This is one of the clearest examples of a "trick" that was actually a bug.

**DAPO: four engineering fixes at scale.** DAPO decouples clipping thresholds (ε_low = 0.2, ε_high = 0.28, "clip-higher") to leave room for low-probability exploration tokens; introduces dynamic sampling that filters prompts with accuracy 0 or 1 so every batch retains a non-zero gradient; switches to token-level policy-gradient loss (critical for long CoT); and applies overlong reward shaping [5][6]. It reached 50 on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-32B versus 47 for DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B, using roughly 50% of the training steps [5]. The paper also documents that a naive GRPO baseline reached only ~30, attributing the gap to entropy collapse, reward noise, and instability [6].

**Sequence-level ratios.** GSPO (Qwen) moves the importance ratio from tokens to sequences, defining s_i(θ) as the length-normalised geometric mean of token ratios with sequence-level clipping [7]. The Qwen blog argues GRPO exhibits severe instability over long training, leading to irreversible collapse, and reports that GSPO clips two orders of magnitude more tokens than GRPO yet trains more efficiently [37]. Its sequence-level design is also more tolerant of numerical discrepancies between inference and training engines, which matters for partial-rollout and multi-turn setups [7]

**Rehabilitating the value model.** VAPO argues the pendulum has swung too far: with value pretraining, decoupled GAE, length-adaptive GAE, clip-higher and token-level loss, a value-based method reached 60.4 on AIME 2024 within 5,000 steps on Qwen2.5-32B — beating DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B (47) and DAPO (50) by more than 10 points with no training crashes [8][38]. This remains an explicit disagreement with the critic-free consensus.

**Baseline alternatives and the "RL-free" branch.** RLOO uses the mean reward of the *other* samples as an unbiased leave-one-out baseline and treats the entire completion as a single action, requiring one fewer model copy and running 2–3× faster than PPO while matching or beating it [9]. REINFORCE++ keeps the critic-free simplicity but replaces the per-prompt baseline with a **global batch mean** to avoid prompt overfitting, with per-token KL and PPO-style clipping [10]. DPO remains the reference point for bypassing RL entirely [33], though for reasoning tasks the online-RL branch has largely won out in practice [35].

**Systems-level cost reduction.** Algorithmic savings are complemented by asynchronous execution: asynchronous RLHF decouples generation from training, tolerating bounded staleness for ~40% speedup on LLaMA-3.1-8B and ~70% on GSM8K finetuning [39]; AReaL uses interruptible rollout workers that swap weights mid-generation, reporting up to 2.77× speedup with matched or improved final performance [40]. The trade-off is explicit off-policyness, which must be corrected for [39][40].

Across every algorithmic axis above — objective granularity, advantage estimation, KL placement, clipping — the published evidence is fragmented: exact apples-to-apples comparisons are rare because base models, data mixes, and step budgets differ. "Tricks or Traps?" responds by reproducing techniques in a unified framework and finds that a minimalist two-technique recipe surpasses GRPO and DAPO, while cautioning that inconsistent settings have generated genuinely conflicting conclusions [36]. Notably, the interaction between clipping and entropy is itself disputed: DAPO frames raising ε_high as *increasing* entropy [5], whereas a later analysis shows clip-high on positive advantages *decreases* entropy, with clip-low increasing it, and that under symmetric clipping the entropy-reducing term dominates [24].

## Reward design: verifiable rewards, process supervision, and reward hacking

**RLVR as the default.** TÜLU 3 coined "Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards" and formalised verifiable reward mechanisms — rule-based or hard-coded checkers producing binary/numeric scores [12]. DeepSeek-R1 adopted exactly this: accuracy rewards (boxed-answer string matching for math; compiler plus test cases for code) combined with format rewards at equal weight, and the authors state they "abstain from applying neural reward models — whether outcome-based or process-based — to reasoning tasks" because such models are susceptible to reward hacking at scale and costly to retrain [11]. Surveys now catalogue reward families as rule-based, model-based judge, format, composite/soft, process-level, and intrinsic (entropy, self-certainty, token probability) [34], with production systems trending toward hybrid stacks that pair hard rule checks with soft judges [13].

**The granularity trade-off: ORM vs PRM.** Outcome reward models score only the final answer and are cheap but sparse; process reward models score every step and are dense but far more expensive to build. Step-level labels come from human annotation (PRM800K), Monte-Carlo rollouts (Math-Shepherd, which auto-constructs step supervision without human labels) [18], formal oracles (Lean tactic verification) [13], or internal-probability methods [13]. A notable efficiency trend is generative PRMs: ThinkPRM, a long-CoT "verbalised" verifier, uses only 1% of the PRM800K process labels yet outperforms discriminative verifiers and LLM-as-a-judge on ProcessBench, MATH-500 and AIME'24, and beats full-label discriminative verifiers out of domain by 8% on GPQA-Diamond and 4.5% on LiveCodeBench [17]. RM-R1 generalises this to "reasoning reward models" that integrate CoT into reward computation [41]. But the analytical survey levels an important caution: PRMs beat ORMs at *test-time selection*, while "the advantage of PRMs is less clear in online RL: outcome-level rewards can outperform PRMs, and PRMs may suffer from severe reward hacking due to their noisier reward signals" [13].

**Reward hacking as a scaling phenomenon.** Over-optimisation is not a corner case but a predictable consequence of optimisation pressure: for direct alignment algorithms the proxy-reward/true-quality gap widens at higher KL budgets, "often before even a single epoch of the dataset is completed" [14]. Ensembles of reward models help — particularly when ensemble members differ in pretraining seed rather than only in fine-tuning seed — but the paper's title is the finding: they mitigate but do not eliminate hacking [15]. Purpose-built testbeds now deliberately separate proxy from true reward, for math/test-harness manipulation and for coding loopholes, to measure hacking rates systematically [42]. On the model-based side, the fragility is stark: a single token can flip an LLM-as-a-judge reward model to a false positive [16], and imperfect verifiers inject noise that requires explicit correction algorithms [19].

**Data curation is a first-class design axis.** Decontaminated, verifiable corpora such as DeepMath-103K [43] treat data construction as part of reward design, since verifiability and cleanliness determine what the reward can even measure. Token-level analysis shows only ~20% high-entropy "minority" tokens materially drive RLVR gains, which reframes credit assignment as a data/signal design problem rather than purely an algorithmic one [44]. Position work argues that many reported gains are overstated once the "RLVR tax" — evaluation pitfalls and contamination — is accounted for [27].

## Emergent behaviours, long CoT, and test-time compute scaling

**What actually emerges.** The canonical result is DeepSeek-R1-Zero's spontaneous development of longer thinking, self-verification, reflection, and an "aha moment" — a sudden surge in "wait" tokens during reflection — purely from outcome-only RL [11]. This is the strongest existing evidence that RL can *elicit* structured reasoning without supervised traces.

**But some of it is surface pattern.** NoWait shows that suppressing explicit reflection markers such as "Wait" and "Hmm" at inference roughly halves CoT length across ten benchmarks while implying limited accuracy loss — suggesting the reflection vocabulary is partly stylistic rather than functionally necessary [45]. Together with the Dr. GRPO length-bias analysis, which shows that response-length normalisation inflates incorrect long responses and can manufacture apparent length growth [4], there is real doubt about how much of "emergent long thinking" is capability versus artifact.

**Longer is not monotonically better.** TOPS finds excessive CoT length *impairs* accuracy, especially on easier tasks, because longer traces accumulate more erroneous steps, and that optimal reasoning effort is task-dependent; it proposes training a tag model with varying efforts and self-improving on shortest-correct responses [46]. This directly contradicts the naive "longer = better" reading of length scaling.

**Test-time compute: mechanisms and limits.** s1's budget forcing lengthens or shortens thinking by manipulating the end-of-thinking token, exceeded o1-preview by up to 27% on competition math, and extrapolated AIME24 from 50% to 57%; sequential scaling outperformed parallel majority voting at matched compute [47]. Snell et al. formalise the parallel-versus-sequential allocation choice, showing compute-optimal allocation beats best-of-N by ~4× and can beat a 14× larger model on easy/medium problems — but *not* on the hardest problems, so test-time and pretraining compute are not 1-to-1 exchangeable [48]. Verification design is the bottleneck for the parallel route; a dedicated survey taxonomises generation/verification/selection choices [49]. Practically, long-context efficiency enables the whole regime: MiniMax-M1's lightning-attention design supports 1M-token context, 8× DeepSeek-R1's, for efficient test-time scaling [50].

**Distillation as a distinct expansion route.** Distillation from a stronger teacher introduces genuinely new reasoning patterns beyond the base model's boundary in a way RLVR does not, in the account of the pass@k critique [20]. But small models struggle to learn from strong reasoners' long CoTs — the "Small Model Learnability Gap" — motivating mixed short/long distillation [51], and RL-learned teachers can bridge the two paradigms by supplying dense step-level rewards for distillation [52].

## Does RL expand reasoning capability or merely reweight it?

This is the field's central and unresolved dispute, and it should be reported as such.

**The reweighting thesis.** "Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model?" evaluates pass@k at large k across model families, RL algorithms, and math/code/visual benchmarks, and finds that RLVR models beat base models at small k but are *overtaken* as k grows, with the reasoning boundary sometimes narrowing during training; perplexity analysis indicates RLVR paths already lie inside the base model's sampling distribution [20]. Six popular RLVR algorithms behave similarly [20], and symbolic multi-instance evaluation of math tasks indicates benchmark overfitting rather than genuine reasoning gains [53].

**The expansion counter-thesis.** ProRL runs RL for over 2,000 steps with KL control, reference-policy resets, and diverse tasks; it reports tasks where the base model fails at *any* sampling budget while the RL model reaches 100% pass, with gains largest exactly where the base model is weakest, and rising trajectory novelty as measured by a Creativity Index [21].

**The reconciliation may be metric-dependent.** CoT-Pass@k requires both a correct reasoning chain and a correct answer; under this stricter metric RLVR *does* incentivise correct reasoning, implying the negative conclusion partly reflects a metric that rewards only final-answer correctness [22].

**The metric itself is flawed at high k.** On tasks with small discrete answer spaces, pass@k necessarily converges to 1 by chance — the base model reaches pass@k = 1 by guessing when there are only ~30 possible outputs and thousands of trials — so breadth-depth metrics such as Cover@τ are proposed instead, and they change the ranking of RL algorithms [31]. Separately, the gradient of a pass@k objective is shown to be merely a positive per-example reweighting of the pass@1 gradient, vanishing exactly when pass@1 ≈ 0 (when exploration is needed most) and saturating as the policy concentrates; pass@k is therefore a diagnostic, not a valid objective [32].

**Saturation as the mechanism.** Diversity collapse can be formalised as overtraining: with n = 8 rollouts, a single success gives an empirical p̂ = 1/8, implying Pass@256 ≈ 1, so most standard RLVR updates contribute nothing to the boundary; restricting updates to problems with zero observed success lifts Pass@256 *above* the base model on difficult benchmarks, and Bayesian Boundary Gating improves average Pass@k across a range of k [23]. This suggests the crossover is real but masked, rather than evidence that RL cannot expand capability.

The measurement critique adds a further layer: pass@k conclusions are only as sound as the evaluation, and at least five premises behind the boundary inference — answer-channel validity, aggregation faithfulness, optimisation neutrality, decoding/estimation control, and scope — currently lack support, with six mutually incompatible mechanistic explanations still undistinguished experimentally [31].

## Evaluation, reproducibility, and open problems

**Confounded headline numbers.** Reported RLVR gains conflate genuine policy improvement with budget mismatch (base evaluated at pass@1 against RLVR at pass@k), attempt inflation and calibration drift (abstentions converted into confident answers), and benchmark contamination; budget-matched reproductions shrink or eliminate several widely cited gaps [27]. Contamination is documented concretely: LiveCodeBench, which tags problems by release date, shows stark drops on post-cutoff problems for several frontier models and finds evidence of overfitting to HumanEval [29]; partial-prompt probing shows Qwen-family models can reconstruct hidden tails on legacy math sets (80% prefix) but not on fresh sets such as AIME-2025 [27]; and a systematic review of 55 detection studies estimates inflation of roughly 6%–40% depending on benchmark and setting, while finding no detection method reliable across contamination tiers [28]. Dynamic, contamination-resistant benchmarks — LiveCodeBench [29] and LiveBench [30] — are the primary mitigation.

**Contamination auditing has a blind spot.** Detecting contamination introduced *during RL post-training* is a distinct problem from pre-training contamination: output entropy distributions collapse into highly specific sparse modes after RL, standard pre-training detectors (PPL, Min-K%, Min-K%++) perform near random in this regime, and an entropy-based Self-Critique probe improves average AUC by up to ~30% [28].

**Instability, entropy collapse, and diversity loss.** RLVR reliably raises pass@1 while degrading high-k pass@k, and the resulting entropy/diversity collapse has spawned a large family of interventions: STEER reweights token-level entropy dynamics [25], CURE uses critical-token-guided re-concatenation to prevent collapse while balancing exploration and exploitation [26], and gradient-level analyses motivate updating only on positive-advantage completions for stability [32]. This connects back to the algorithmic layer: clipping choices are entropy choices [24], and entropy-control schedules are increasingly treated as a first-class hyperparameter [25][26].

**Reproducibility.** Training is highly sensitive to technique and hyperparameter choices, and inconsistent experimental settings, data, and initialisation have produced conflicting published conclusions; unified reproductions such as "Tricks or Traps?" identify which techniques genuinely help and show a minimalist combination can beat GRPO and DAPO [36]. The broader surveys also note that recipe details (KL placement, normalisation, clipping) are frequently under-specified, making cross-paper comparison unreliable [34][35].

**Safety and scope.** Whether reasoning-oriented RL erodes safety alignment is unsettled: one line of work argues RLVR maintains safety guardrails while improving verifiable-domain performance, countering the assumed safety–capability trade-off [54], while reasoning on subjective, non-verifiable tasks introduces distinct failure modes that the RLVR paradigm does not address [13].

**Open problems.**

1. *No operational definition of "reasoning boundary."* The expand-vs-reweight debate cannot be resolved until the construct is measurable, and no accepted definition exists [31].
2. *Untested reconciliations.* Six incompatible mechanistic accounts of the pass@k crossover remain experimentally undistinguished; head-to-head tests under matched budgets are the obvious next experiment [31][32].
3. *Reward hacking under optimisation pressure.* Ensembles mitigate but do not eliminate hacking [15], over-optimisation appears even within a single epoch [14], and judge-based rewards remain one-token fragile [16]; no robust general defence exists.
4. *The cost and role of process supervision.* Data-efficient generative PRMs narrow the labelling cost dramatically [17], but whether PRMs help in *online* RL (as opposed to test-time search) is actively contested [13].
5. *Verifier reliability and calibration.* Rule-based and model-based rewards are not commensurable scalars, and imperfect verifiers inject noise requiring explicit correction [19][13].
6. *Contamination-free evaluation of post-training.* RL-phase contamination auditing is immature relative to pre-training auditing, and dynamic benchmarks only partially cover the space [28][29][30].
7. *Generalisation beyond math and code.* Most long-CoT and test-time-scaling evidence is math-heavy; extension to open-ended and agentic domains is asserted more often than rigorously demonstrated [46][13].

## Trends and open problems

**Trend 1: convergence on critic-free group-relative RL, then partial pushback.** Two years ago the default was PPO with a learned value function [1]; today GRPO-style methods dominate open recipes [2][5][34], with DAPO's four fixes and Dr. GRPO's debiasing becoming standard practice [5][4][6]. Yet the value model has not been abandoned: VAPO's 60.4 AIME24 with value pretraining [8] is a direct challenge, and GSPO's sequence-level ratios [7][37] indicate the objective is still under active redesign rather than settled.

**Trend 2: reward design moving from learned models to verifiers — and back toward hybrids.** The defining shift is DeepSeek-R1's explicit refusal of neural reward models for reasoning [11] enabled by RLVR-style rule checkers [12]. The current frontier is hybrid: rule checks where ground truth exists [13], generative and reasoning reward models where it does not [41][17], and rubric-based rewards for open-ended tasks [13]. The unresolved tension is that model-based rewards are the most flexible and the most hackable [16][15].

**Trend 3: the debate moving from "does it work" to "how do we measure it."** The most-cited recent results are methodological critiques rather than new SOTA numbers: the pass@k inversion [20], metric-invalidity analyses [31][32], overtraining/saturation accounts [23], and the RLVR measurement-gap position paper [27]. This is a healthy maturation but it means that many headline gains currently in circulation should be treated as provisional.

**Trend 4: efficiency and systems becoming first-class contributions.** Asynchronous and partial-rollout RL [39][40], long-context attention designs for long reasoning traces [50], and gradient-stabilising objectives [32][25] are increasingly reported as core results rather than implementation details, because rollout cost is the practical ceiling on RL scale.

**Disputed results, stated plainly.** (a) Whether RL expands reasoning boundaries: yes per [21][22][23], no per [20][53]. (b) Whether value-based RL beats value-free: yes per [8][38], no per [2][5][9]. (c) Whether raising the upper clipping threshold increases or decreases entropy: opposite conclusions in [5] versus [24]. (d) Whether reflection tokens like "Wait" are functionally necessary [11] or removable stylistic artifacts [45]. (e) Whether PRMs help in online RL [13][17]. Each of these is a live disagreement in the 2024–2026 literature, and a responsible reading of the field requires reporting both sides rather than a single leaderboard.
