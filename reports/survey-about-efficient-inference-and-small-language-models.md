# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Survey of Techniques, Evidence, and Open Problems

## TL;DR

- **"Small" is a moving target, not a parameter count.** Surveys of the field explicitly refuse to fix a threshold, noting that GPT-2's 1.5B parameters made it a *large* model in 2019 while sub-4B models are deployed as production "small" models today; SLMs are better defined by the resource envelope they must fit into than by raw size [1][2][3].
- **Overtraining on curated data, plus distillation, is the dominant recipe.** SmolLM2-1.7B was trained on ~11T tokens and claims to beat Qwen2.5-1.5B and Llama-3.2-1B [4]; Phi-3-mini (3.8B) reaches 69% MMLU largely through heavily filtered web plus synthetic data [5]; Gemma 2's 2B/9B models were distilled from a larger teacher on >50× compute-optimal token budgets [6]; and Llama 3.2 1B/3B are one-shot structured prunings of Llama 3.1 8B with logit distillation from 8B/70B teachers [7].
- **Compression is now genuinely near-lossless at 4 bits, but the win is bandwidth, not FLOPs.** GPTQ and AWQ deliver near-lossless 4-bit weights with ~3–4× end-to-end speedups and >3× on-device speedups respectively [8][9], while rotation-based QuaRot/SpinQuant close 4-bit weight+activation+KV-cache quantization to a ~2.9-point zero-shot gap on LLaMA-2 7B [10][11]. Ternary/1.58-bit weights can match FP16 at equal size and tokens if the model is *trained* that way rather than compressed afterwards [12][13].
- **The KV cache, not the matrix multiply, is the binding constraint in serving.** Naive serving systems wasted 60–80% of KV memory and a single LLaMA-13B sequence's cache could reach ~1.7 GB, which PagedAttention reduced to near-zero waste while enabling up to 24× throughput over naive serving [14][15]; MLA cut DeepSeek-V2's KV cache by 93.3% and raised throughput 5.76× [16]; and 2-bit KV quantization (KIVI) reduces peak memory 2.6× for 2.35–3.47× throughput [17].
- **Speculative decoding and test-time compute both have real ceilings that the 2024–2026 literature is now measuring.** EAGLE-3 reaches up to 6.5× speedup but only ~1.38× at batch size 64 [18], and while compute-optimal test-time scaling can let a 1B model beat a 405B model on MATH-500 [19], inverse-scaling and overthinking studies show longer reasoning *degrades* accuracy on some tasks [20][21].

## Background

Efficient inference and small language models (SLMs) are two halves of the same engineering problem: how to obtain useful language capability at a cost — in memory, latency, energy, or dollars — that makes deployment practical. The two literatures converge because the levers are shared: a smaller model reduces the cost of every token, and every inference optimization increases the capability a given model size can deliver.

The topic matters now for three reasons. First, capability per parameter has risen sharply: Phi-3-mini at 3.8B parameters reports 69% MMLU and 8.38 MT-Bench, rivalling Mixtral 8×7B and GPT-3.5 [5]. Second, deployment has moved to the edge: Llama 3.2's 1B and 3B models were shipped with day-one Qualcomm and MediaTek enablement and Arm optimizations [7], and Apple's on-device foundation model runs at roughly 30 tokens/s on an iPhone 15 Pro [22][23]. Third, the economics of inference now dominate training for high-volume applications, which is why so much of the field's energy has shifted from "train a bigger model" to "serve the model you have more cheaply."

This report synthesizes four bodies of evidence: SLM families and training recipes [1][2][3], compression and low-precision inference [8][9][24], decoding-time and serving-system acceleration [14][16][25], and inference-time compute scaling, edge deployment, and routing [26][19][22][27]. A recurring theme is that the field's headline numbers are frequently measured under favourable conditions — batch size 1, templated tasks, uncontaminated benchmarks — and the more recent literature is largely a systematic accounting of where those numbers fail to transfer.

## Small language model families and training recipes

**Definitional ambiguity is a substantive finding, not a quibble.** The two major SLM surveys both decline to fix a parameter threshold [1][2]. One frames SLMs by an "inherent difficulty": small and large are functions of context and time, and an SLM is a model that retains LLM-level accuracy and adaptability under some constraint on hardware, data, bandwidth, or latency [1]. The other defines SLMs by capability on specialized tasks subject to resource constraints, bounded below by the minimum size at which task abilities emerge and above by the largest model sustainable in the target environment [2]. A third survey takes an empirical route, measuring the properties, costs, and inference characteristics of small models as a population rather than arguing about taxonomy [3]. Operationally, the literature clusters around sub-7B and frequently sub-4B models.

**A first wave of small open models established the template.** Gemma shipped 2B and 7B models trained on up to 6T tokens and outperforming similarly sized open models on 11 of 18 text benchmarks [28]; Qwen2.5's open weights span 0.5B through 72B, pretrained on up to 18T tokens with 128K context, with Qwen2.5-0.5B reported to outperform Gemma2-2.6B on various math and coding tasks [29]; and MobileLLM showed that for sub-billion models architecture matters more than raw parameter count, with deep-and-thin designs, embedding sharing, and grouped-query attention adding 2.7–4.3% over prior 125M/350M state of the art, and MobileLLM-350M approaching LLaMA-v2 7B on API-calling intent and structure exact match [30].

**Data curation is the single most emphasized lever.** The phi line established the thesis that synthetic, textbook-quality data can substitute for scale [31][32]. Phi-3-mini's technical report states that the innovation "lies entirely in our dataset" — heavily filtered public web text plus synthetic data — rather than architecture [5]. SmolLM2 is explicitly data-centric: it introduces FineMath, Stack-Edu, and SmolTalk because existing datasets were "problematically small or low-quality," uses a staged mixture that shifts from mostly web to code/math-heavy as training progresses, and rebalances data mixtures online based on benchmark signals rather than running multiple full-scale training runs [4]. SmolLM3 extends this with a three-stage curriculum followed by mid-training on 140B reasoning tokens and dual `/think` and `/no_think` modes [33].

**Overtraining past compute-optimal is now standard, and it is predictable.** TinyLlama trained a 1.1B model on up to ~3T tokens — far beyond the Chinchilla-optimal point for that size — and beat OPT-1.3B and Pythia-1.4B [34]. SmolLM2's 1.7B model consumed ~11T tokens at a reported cost of roughly $250,000 of GPU compute [4]. Crucially, the scaling literature has validated this: over-training scaling laws fitted on a large testbed predict the loss of a deliberately over-trained 1.4B/900B-token run to within 0.7% relative error, with a token multiplier a useful empirical metric [35]. Kaplan–Chinchilla discrepancies were traced to experimental artefacts (last-layer compute cost, warmup duration, scale-dependent optimizer tuning), which is important because it means "compute-optimal" was never a fixed law of nature [36].

**Distillation and pruning increasingly produce the small models rather than training them from scratch.** Gemma 2's 2B and 9B models used knowledge distillation from a larger teacher and were trained on more than 50× the compute-optimal token quantity, on the argument that small models remain under-trained [6]. Llama 3.2 1B/3B were produced by one-shot structured pruning from Llama 3.1 8B, with logits from the 8B and 70B models used as token-level targets during pretraining [7]. For reasoning specifically, Mentor-KD inserts an intermediate "mentor" model to supply additional chain-of-thought annotations and soft labels, improving multi-step reasoning distillation into small students [37]. A general finding across this work is that distillation is most valuable exactly where the teacher's advantage is largest: structured, multi-step, format-sensitive tasks [6][7][37].

**Where small models still fail is now well characterized.** Phi-3-mini's own model card is candid that the model is "fundamentally limited by its size" and weak on world knowledge, citing TriviaQA 64.0 and recommending search augmentation as a mitigation [38]. Small models struggle with natural-language arithmetic due to surface ambiguity, improving markedly when problems are reformatted as equations [39]. Gemma 2's 2B model is measurably less format-robust than its larger siblings, with higher benchmark variance [6]. A survey of reasoning in SLMs concludes that small models can reach competitive reasoning through structured training and compression, but that the gap is task-dependent rather than uniform [40]. The natural bridge to deployment is to stop asking small models to be small large models and instead give them tools: replacing internal chain-of-thought with tool calls has been shown to enable reasoning behaviour in small models that they cannot produce unaided [41].

## Compression: quantization, pruning, and distillation

**Weight-only 4-bit post-training quantization is close to a solved problem.** GPTQ uses approximate second-order (Hessian-based) information to quantize very large models in one shot — 175B parameters in roughly four GPU-hours — with negligible degradation at 3–4 bits, and reports ~3.25× speedup on an A100 and ~4.5× on an A6000 versus FP16 [8]. AWQ's insight is that protecting only about 1% of salient weight channels, identified via *activation* rather than weight magnitude, drastically reduces quantization error; via its TinyChat runtime it reports >3× speedup over FP16 and runs Llama-2-70B on a mobile GPU [9]. SqueezeLLM attacks the same problem with sensitivity-based non-uniform quantization and a dense-and-sparse decomposition storing outliers in full precision, claiming lossless compression to 3 bits and up to 2.3× speedup [42]. The practical consequence is that 4-bit weight-only inference is the default deployment configuration, with the residual difficulty moving to activations, KV cache, and extreme low bit-widths. The deployment picture is corroborated by serving frameworks: vLLM's documented quantization support covers AWQ, GPTQ/Marlin, INT8 W8A8, INT4 W4A16, FP8 W8A8, and a quantized KV-cache path, with explicit hardware gating — INT8 W8A8 requires compute capability above 7.5 and is unsupported on Blackwell-class hardware, where FP8 is recommended instead [43].

**Activation outliers, not weights, are the real obstacle below 8 bits.** SmoothQuant migrates activation-outlier difficulty into the weights via an equivalent mathematical transform, enabling training-free W8A8 INT8 with up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction, sufficient to fit a 530B model on a single node [24]. OmniQuant generalizes this with learnable weight clipping and a learnable equivalent transformation, reaching W4A4 and even W2A16 configurations in 1–16 hours on a single A100 with 128 calibration samples [44]. Rotation-based methods go further by removing the outlier structure rather than rescaling it: QuaRot applies computational-invariance rotations to the residual stream, attention/FFN activations, and KV cache, achieving end-to-end 4-bit inference with at most 0.47 WikiText-2 perplexity loss and 99% zero-shot retention on Llama 2-70B, and lossless results at 6- and 8-bit with no calibration data [10]. SpinQuant shows the rotation itself should be learned: random rotations can differ by up to 13 points of zero-shot accuracy, and learned rotations shrink the 4-bit weight/activation/KV gap to 2.9 points on LLaMA-2 7B, beating LLM-QAT by 19.1 points and SmoothQuant by 25.0 [11]. Notably, SpinQuant also reports that LLaMA-3 8B is "hard to quantize" — evidence that quantizability depends on architecture and training data, not purely on size.

**Extreme low bit-width works best when it is trained for, not applied afterwards.** BitNet b1.58 stores every weight as ternary {-1, 0, 1} (about 1.58 bits) and matches full-precision Transformers of identical size and training-token budget on perplexity and end tasks, while reducing latency, memory, throughput, and energy costs, and defines a new scaling behaviour [12]. BitNet a4.8 adds 4-bit activations for attention and FFN inputs while sparsifying intermediate states, matching b1.58 at equal training cost, activating only 55% of parameters, and supporting 3-bit KV cache [13]. BitNet v2 uses an online Hadamard transform to enable *native* 4-bit activations with performance comparable to 8-bit activations [45]. Deployment tooling has followed: bitnet.cpp targets fast, lossless ternary inference on commodity CPUs [46]. The pattern is clear — below 4 bits, quantization becomes an architectural decision made during training rather than a post-hoc compression step [12][13][45].

**Pruning and structured compression are complementary and remarkably cheap.** SparseGPT performs one-shot pruning of 50–60% of weights on OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B in under 4.5 hours with negligible perplexity increase, and extends to 2:4 and 4:8 semi-structured patterns that map to hardware [47]. Wanda is simpler still, requiring no retraining or weight update, pruned by per-output |weight| × input-activation magnitude, and beating magnitude pruning [48]. Structured methods target dimensions rather than individual weights: LLM-Pruner prunes coupled structures using gradient information and heals with LoRA in about three hours on 50K samples [49], while SliceGPT deletes rows and columns to reduce embedding dimension, removing up to 25% of parameters for Llama-2 70B, OPT 66B, and Phi-2 while retaining 99%/99%/90% of zero-shot performance and cutting inference compute on Llama-2 70B to 64% [50]. Layer pruning is also viable — up to roughly half of the deeper layers in some open models can be removed before collapse, using angular-similarity layer selection plus QLoRA healing [51]. For distillation, the on-policy line of work is the most important conceptual shift: MiniLLM and GKD both identify the train/inference distribution mismatch of sequence-level knowledge distillation and fix it by training the student on its own self-generated sequences with teacher feedback [52][53], and this recipe has been applied directly to post-training small models [54].

**A critical caveat: precision interacts with the scaling recipe, and quantization degradation is not a function of size alone.** Precision-aware scaling laws, fitted on hundreds of runs, show that quantization degradation grows as models are trained on more data — meaning additional pretraining data can become actively harmful under aggressive quantization [55]. This is a direct challenge to the intuition that efficiency techniques are free add-ons to a scaling recipe, and it matters most for exactly the over-trained small models that dominate the SLM literature [4][34][35]. Meanwhile, KV-cache compression continues to move into the memory wall: rematerializing the KV cache rather than storing it has been proposed as a way to break the memory bottleneck [56].

## Decoding-time acceleration: KV cache, speculation, and serving systems

**Decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound, and the KV cache is why.** Autoregressive decoding produces one token per step while re-reading the entire model and the full key/value history from HBM, so throughput scales with achievable bandwidth divided by bytes-per-token rather than with FLOPs. vLLM's team reported that a single LLaMA-13B sequence's KV cache can consume ~1.7 GB, and that naive serving systems wasted 60–80% of KV memory to fragmentation and over-reservation [15]. PagedAttention addressed this by partitioning the cache into fixed-size blocks mapped through a block table, with copy-on-write sharing — achieving near-zero KV memory waste, enabling memory sharing that substantially reduces the footprint of parallel sampling and beam search, and delivering up to 24× throughput over HuggingFace Transformers [14][57][15].

**Architectural fixes shrink the cache structurally; system fixes shrink it dynamically.** On the architecture side, Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) in DeepSeek-V2 compresses the KV cache into a low-rank latent vector, cutting cache size by 93.3% and raising maximum generation throughput 5.76× relative to DeepSeek 67B at 128K context [16]; DeepSeek-V3 continues the MLA-plus-MoE line at 671B total / 37B activated parameters [58]. On the system side, KIVI exploits the observation that keys should be quantized per-channel while values should be quantized per-token, giving a tuning-free 2-bit scheme with 2.6× lower peak memory, up to 4× larger batch size, and 2.35–3.47× throughput [17]. Token eviction and compression methods — ChunkKV, RocketKV, Ada-KV, and related work — exploit attention sparsity, with RocketKV using a two-stage compression scheme for long context [59][60] and Ada-KV allocating eviction budgets adaptively per head rather than uniformly [61].

**But KV compression is not uniformly benign — a result that is increasingly being foregrounded.** A 2026 study of chunked KV-cache compression identifies systematic, position-dependent "phase" weaknesses: chunked compression introduces periodic retrieval failures rather than graceful uniform degradation [62]. This is a good example of the field's maturing methodology: negative and second-order effects that were previously buried in average benchmark scores are now the paper's contribution. The practical guidance that follows is that compression ratios reported as averages should be validated against long-context retrieval benchmarks rather than assumed to transfer [59][60][62].

**Speculative decoding: output-preserving acceleration with a hard batch-size ceiling.** Speculative decoding uses a cheap approximation model to draft several tokens in parallel and then verifies them against the target model with a procedure that provably preserves the target distribution, giving 2–3× acceleration on T5-XXL with identical outputs and no retraining [25]. Medusa replaces the draft model with multiple parallel decoding heads over the base model, verified with tree attention [63]. The EAGLE line drafts in feature space (EAGLE), improves the draft tree (EAGLE-2), and then drops feature prediction for direct token prediction with multi-layer feature fusion and a "training-time test," reaching up to 6.5× speedup with average acceptance length up to 7.5 [18]. Self-speculative approaches avoid a second model entirely: Draft & Verify skips intermediate layers to form the draft, reporting ~1.73× speedup with no extra model or training [64], and SWIFT does this adaptively on the fly [65].

The ceiling matters more than the peak. Acceptance rate is strongly task-dependent — EAGLE-3 performs best on templated code tasks and worst on high-entropy generation — and the drafts must be substantially cheaper than the target. Critically, EAGLE's own throughput behaviour changes with batch size: the original EAGLE degrades at larger batches, whereas EAGLE-3 reports a 1.38× throughput improvement at batch size 64 in the SGLang framework, holding gains well beyond the batch sizes where speculation was previously assumed to be pointless [18]. That discrepancy between generations of the same method family is itself the finding: speculative decoding was long assumed to be unhelpful at large batch, and the current answer is that it can work there, but only with substantially better drafting. Production evidence is consistent: NVIDIA reports 3.55×/3.16×/2.63× throughput speedups on a single H200 for Llama 3.2 1B/3B and Llama 3.1 8B drafts accelerating Llama 3.3 70B (181.74 vs 51.14 tokens/s baseline) [66].

**Serving systems are where the gains compound, and scheduling is the lever.** Continuous (iteration-level) batching schedules per token rather than per request; chunked prefill mixes compute-bound prefill with memory-bound decode in a single batch to improve inter-token latency and GPU utilization, and is now the default in vLLM V1 with a recommendation of `max_num_batched_tokens` > 8192 for throughput (smaller values favour latency) [67][68]. Prefill/decode disaggregation goes further by placing the compute-bound prefill and memory-bound decode phases on separate hardware: DistServe optimizes goodput under latency constraints [69], Sarathi-Serve combines chunked prefills with stall-free scheduling to tame the throughput–latency tradeoff [70], and Nexus adapts the resource split intra-GPU [71]. Not every proposal wins — vAttention argued for dynamic memory management *without* PagedAttention [72] — and 2026 work is pushing toward phase-specialized quantization that treats prefill and decode differently [73] and toward serving systems for token-level model routing [74]. The overall lesson is that a serving stack is a portfolio: paging [14], chunked prefill [67], disaggregation [69], quantized KV [17], and speculation [18] each address a different bottleneck, and their benefits only partially compound.

## Inference-time compute scaling: a substitute for parameters with a sharp ceiling

**Test-time compute can substitute for parameters, at least on some tasks.** The s1 recipe is the simplest reproducible demonstration: curate a 1,000-question dataset (s1K) selected for difficulty, diversity, and quality, then apply "budget forcing" — forcing the model to stop thinking, or appending "Wait" to make it keep going — to induce self-checking. SFT of Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on s1K with budget forcing yields a model that exceeds o1-preview by up to 27% on competition math, and extrapolating inference compute lifts AIME24 from 50% to 57% without further training [26]. The compute-optimal follow-up makes the claim sharper: with a properly tuned test-time strategy, a 1B model can surpass a 405B model on MATH-500, a 0.5B model can beat GPT-4o, and a 7B model can beat o1 and DeepSeek-R1 [19]. Efficient tree search has been proposed to reduce the memory cost of such scaling [75].

**But the substitution is not monotone, and this is the most important recent correction.** An inverse-scaling study constructs tasks across four categories — counting with distractors, regression with spurious features, deduction with constraint tracking, and advanced-AI-risk scenarios — where *longer* reasoning degrades accuracy, and identifies five distinct failure modes. Concretely, DeepSeek R1's accuracy on a counting task collapses from 70% to 30% as distractors are added, and Claude Sonnet 4's expressed willingness to be shut off falls from 60% to 47% as reasoning length grows (these specific percentages are reported in the paper's results tables rather than its abstract) [20]. A complementary overthinking study finds that on GPQA-Diamond both R1-32B and s1-32B peak near ~10K generated tokens and then decline, with the ratio of negative to positive answer flips exceeding 1.0 at 7K tokens and robustly above 1 at 8K; cost-aware stopping at ~6K tokens saves roughly 50% of compute for about 6% accuracy loss [21].

Two implications deserve emphasis. First, the "more thinking is always better" heuristic is empirically false, and the failure is not confined to toy tasks — it appears in safety-relevant behaviours [20]. Second, techniques that increase reasoning length can *increase* overthinking susceptibility: s1-32B flips earlier (~5K tokens) than R1-32B (~7K), suggesting that SFT-induced budget forcing is a double-edged instrument [21]. Efficient inference and test-time scaling therefore interact rather than compose: the compute you spend on reasoning must itself be budgeted.

## Sub-quadratic architectures and edge deployment

**Sub-quadratic sequence models trade a specific capability for linear cost.** State-space and linear-attention models such as Mamba and RWKV replace the Transformer's growing per-token memory with a fixed-size recurrent state, giving O(1) per-token state and sub-quadratic scaling. The disputed core is copying and retrieval. "Repeat After Me" proves that a two-layer transformer can copy strings of exponential length, whereas any generalized state-space model with a fixed-size latent state is fundamentally bounded; empirically, pretrained transformers dramatically outperform SSMs at copying and retrieving from context [76]. The pragmatic resolution in the 2024–2026 literature is hybridization: Jamba interleaves Transformer and Mamba layers with mixture-of-experts [77], and Samba combines SSMs with sliding-window attention for efficient unlimited-context modelling with better memory recall than either pure attention or pure SSMs [78]. Follow-on work continues to explore growing-state and dynamic linear attention [79].

**On-device deployment has concrete, bandwidth-driven numbers.** Apple's AFM-on-device is a ~3B-parameter model (about 2.58B non-embedding parameters, 26 layers, grouped-query attention with 24 query and 8 KV heads, shared input/output embeddings) using mixed 2-bit/4-bit palletized weight quantization averaging 3.7 bits per weight, delivering roughly 30 tokens/s and ~0.6 ms per token time-to-first-token on an iPhone 15 Pro [22][23]. The 2025 update moves to 2-bit weights via quantization-aware training, quantizes the KV cache to 8 bits, and uses a two-block depth split with KV-cache sharing to reduce KV memory and prefill latency by about 37.5% [80]. The broader edge design space — architecture choice, compression, and hardware acceleration, with tight memory and energy budgets — is surveyed comprehensively in the on-device literature [81], and function-calling agents have been demonstrated at the edge competitive with much larger hosted models [82].

## Routing, cascades, and small models in agentic pipelines

The practical alternative to choosing one model size is to choose per query. Cascades query cheap models first and escalate only when needed, and learned routers such as RouteLLM use human preference data to route between a strong and a weak model, cutting cost by more than 2× without sacrificing quality and generalizing to model pairs unseen in training [27]. The economic motive is large: the price spread between a small and a frontier model can exceed 50× per million output tokens [27].

Routing has structural limits that the recent literature is beginning to quantify. Routers plateau — gains saturate as more candidate models are added — and router and small model can fail on correlated hard inputs, creating a co-failure ceiling that no amount of routing fixes [27][74]. Escalation also requires a confidence signal, which typically means an LLM-as-judge call whose own cost must be counted. The 2026 direction is to push routing down into the serving system itself, exploiting the fact that a routed conversation keeps state locally and can avoid re-prefilling shared context [74]. In parallel, hybrid architectures are being applied specifically to enterprise reasoning workloads to raise throughput with minimal reasoning-quality degradation [79].

## Trends and open problems

**Trend 1: from "can it be done" to "under what conditions does it hold."** The 2022–2023 efficiency literature reported peak speedups and average accuracy deltas. The 2024–2026 literature increasingly reports the boundaries: speculative decoding's batch-size ceiling [18], KV compression's position-dependent failure modes [62], test-time compute's non-monotonicity [20][21], and routing's plateau [27][74]. This is a healthy maturation, but it makes cross-paper comparison harder, because headline numbers are conditioned on batch size, task entropy, and benchmark choice.

**Trend 2: quantization is becoming a training-time property rather than a post-hoc fix.** The strongest extreme-low-bit results — BitNet b1.58, BitNet a4.8, BitNet v2 — all involve training or continued pretraining in the quantized regime [12][13][45], and Apple's move to 2-bit on-device weights likewise used quantization-aware training [80]. Meanwhile precision-aware scaling laws show that quantization degradation grows with pretraining data, so the choice of precision is not separable from the choice of training budget [55]. The open question is whether over-trained 4-bit and 2-bit models follow the same scaling laws as their full-precision counterparts; current evidence points to "not straightforwardly" [55].

**Trend 3: efficiency is a portfolio, and its components only partially compound.** Paging [14], chunked prefill [67], disaggregation [69], KV quantization [17], speculative decoding [18], and model routing [27] each address a distinct bottleneck, and the empirical record shows diminishing or even zero returns when they are stacked or applied outside their operating regime. A recurring failure pattern is applying a technique at the wrong batch size, sequence length, or task entropy.

**Open problem: unreliable evaluation.** Contamination materially inflates reported capability. N-gram decontamination is insufficient, and a 13B model trained on rephrased test data reached GPT-4-level reported scores (MMLU 45.3 → 88.5; GSM-8K 28.7 → 95.3), with 8–18% HumanEval overlap found in widely used pretraining sets [83]. Inference-time decontamination cuts inflated GSM8K accuracy by 22.9% and MMLU by 19.0%, lowering Phi-3 by 6.7% and Mistral by 3.6% on MMLU [84]. Since small-model capability claims are precisely where benchmark deltas are smallest and most fragile [3][5], contamination risk is highest exactly where the field's most-cited results live. A related gap is that inverse-scaling phenomena do not appear on standard arithmetic benchmarks (MultiArith, ASDiv, GSM8K) or on the original Inverse Scaling Prize datasets, requiring purpose-built stress tests [20].

**Open problem: is the "small model" thesis in tension with the bitter lesson?** The dominant empirical trend of the last two years is that data curation, distillation, and overtraining let small models match much larger predecessors [5][6][7][4]. But the counter-current is equally clear: small models remain fundamentally limited by capacity on knowledge-intensive and compositional tasks [38][39], quantization penalizes overtraining [55], and test-time scaling — the main mechanism for closing the gap at inference — itself degrades on some tasks [20][21]. The honest summary is that parameter count has become a *design variable* rather than a capability ceiling, and the field has not yet produced a unified account of when spending parameters beats spending tokens, precision, or reasoning steps.

**Open problem: reproducibility and reporting standards.** Because SLM results depend jointly on data mixture, token budget, quantization scheme, and serving configuration [4][35][55][18], and because several of the strongest claims come from model cards and vendor blogs rather than peer-reviewed comparisons [38][7][66], the field lacks a shared protocol for reporting efficiency. Until peak and sustained numbers are reported under a standard set of batch sizes, sequence lengths, and task entropies, the efficiency literature will continue to overstate what transfers.
